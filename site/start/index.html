What am I looking at? · Method & limitations
A visibility is a complex correlation between two antennas, not a photograph or an audio stream. START fetches the latest visibility record, antenna positions, station information, and available gain metadata through a read-only relay. Requests are shared for 30 seconds; the desk refreshes every 60 seconds while visible.
The preview uses equal-weight direct Fourier synthesis, Re Σ Vᵢⱼ exp[−2πi (rᵢ − rⱼ)·s / λ], with station antenna positions interpreted in TART's East/North/Up convention. Zenith is the center; north is up and east is right. The inverse phase sign is checked against TART's simulated-visibility convention. Array alignment still depends on the station's supplied position calibration.
When enabled and available, gain/phase correction follows TART: V′ᵢⱼ = Vᵢⱼ gᵢ gⱼ exp[−i(φᵢ−φⱼ)]. Zero-gain antennas and flagged baselines are excluded. Missing or invalid gain metadata produces an explicitly labeled raw preview. This does not establish the age or quality of the upstream calibration. No primary-beam correction, deconvolution, flux calibration, or automatic detection is applied. The matrix continues to show raw measured correlations.
Common-object positions use SIMBAD ICRS coordinates and Astropy's built-in solar-system ephemeris at the observation timestamp and telescope location, with offline Earth-rotation data and no refraction. Satellite predictions come from the TART catalogue for that same time. Markers indicate where to examine data, not what has been detected. Orion is the Orion Nebula (M42), not the full constellation. Catalogue flux/range placeholders are not presented as measured values.
“Fresh” means the observation timestamp is within five minutes of Sol's retrieval time. An API can respond successfully with old data. Failed refreshes retain the previous observation with an explicit warning. Networked TART stations are separate arrays; this page does not coherently combine their signals into a planet-sized interferometer.
05 / A different way to see
SIGNAL → CORRELATION → IMAGE
↘ ↙
Many small antennas.
TART uses an array of radio receivers to sample the same sky. At the GPS L1 band, satellites and local radio interference are prominent parts of the observing environment.
∿ × ∿
One shared measurement.
A correlator compares each antenna pair. A 24-element array provides 276 unique cross-correlations, carrying information about the spatial structure of incoming signals.
⊙
An image made in software.
Antenna geometry and complex correlations let software reconstruct a sky response. Reliable interpretation also needs calibration, interference checks, and the array's response pattern.

06 / Start small. Keep listening.
YOUR PATH INTO RADIO ASTRONOMY
01
Explore a real array, today
Choose a telescope above, compare visibility amplitudes and phases, and download a timestamped JSON snapshot. No receiver or account is needed to read these public observations.

SOFTWARE FIRST
02
Build a single-element receiver
Use an SDR, suitable antenna, filtered low-noise amplifier, and computer. A 21 cm hydrogen-line experiment operates near 1420 MHz, distinct from TART's L1 band. Results depend on the antenna, receiver sensitivity, interference, and integration time.
CHART learning project ↗ · GNU Radio ↗
LEARN THE SIGNAL
03
Try a synchronized interferometer
Compare two or more receivers with a coherent reference and measured delays. A shared 1 PPS alone does not guarantee phase-coherent sampling. Establish clock, local-oscillator, and cable-delay stability before interpreting fringes.
TART documentation & community ↗
MEASURE TOGETHER
04
Build an array. Join a network.
Study current TART hardware, correlator, installation, and calibration designs. Confirm kit availability and a complete bill of materials with the project. Geographic networking enables shared observations; coherent long-baseline imaging needs additional timing, signal, and calibration infrastructure.
